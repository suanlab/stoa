\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

For every item an agent remembers, three coupled questions (in what representation, on which tier, and
when to materialize it) are today answered separately by three research communities. We offered a single
budget-constrained formulation as a frame and measured one of its axes, placement across tiers, on
production KV traces.

The finding is about when to decide, and it is first a finding about evaluation. Evaluated at a split
instant, as is natural, more than nine-tenths of a hindsight oracle's advantage is out of any policy's
reach, whatever the split and whatever the cost weights; inside that protocol a better ranker helps
monotonically, but over a sliver. Deciding at each block's arrival puts most of the advantage within
reach: even first-come-first-served admission captures three-quarters or more of it on both production
traces. Which arrival-time rule is best we cannot yet say, because in a finite evaluation window arrival
order is entangled with exposure time. Reactive tiering, forecasting nothing, already delivers much of the
achievable hit rate, and the adaptive-replacement family adds little to it, because the signal it adapts on
is scarce when most blocks are never reused.

We arrived here only after removing twenty-four measurement defects, each of which had produced a
plausible result, including leaks in our own arrival-time experiment that an audit found after every
number check had passed. We report their mechanisms and the controls that catch them, because the same
traps are open to anyone measuring a memory scheduler. The next steps are an evaluation with equal
exposure per block, a cost model calibrated against real tiers, and the habit of asking what a reported
fraction's denominator could have been.
