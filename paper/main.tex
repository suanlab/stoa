\begin{abstract}
How a memory system for LLM agents should place KV-cache blocks across storage tiers is usually
evaluated at a split instant: observe a prefix, place the blocks that exist, and bill the result
against a hindsight oracle. On two production Mooncake traces we show that this protocol measures
almost nothing. Of the oracle's advantage, 94.7\% and 95.9\% sits on blocks that do not yet exist at
the split, so any causal policy is confined to the remaining 5\% and looks flat however well it ranks;
the share is 92--99\% at every split from 0.3 to 0.7 and is worse at earlier ones. Moving the decision
to each block's arrival raises the reachable share from 5\% to 84\% on one trace and from 4\% to 19\%
on the other. There, first-come-first-served admission captures 87\% and 50\% of what is reachable,
and a learned arrival-time ranker captures less on both, while the arrival order itself is worth up to
14 points. On Mooncake's synthetic control, where only a quarter of blocks are new at the split, the
unreachable share is lower and timing recovers nothing --- the same diagnostic predicts which case a
workload is in. Purely reactive tiering tells the same story from the other side: LRU, LFU and a fill-once
cache reach 75--99\% and 40--94\% of Belady's hit rate, the adaptive-replacement family (ARC, LeCaR,
CACHEUS) adds at most 4.6 points, and a learned replacement policy (LRB) stays below ARC at every
operating point, because 76--78\% of blocks are never reused and the signals all of them adapt on are
scarce. We give a one-line diagnostic for the protocol failure and the correction that removes it, and
release an artifact that regenerates every number on CPU, together with the catalogue of measurement
defects we made and caught along the way.
\end{abstract}
