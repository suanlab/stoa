\begin{abstract}
A natural way to evaluate how a memory system for LLM agents should place KV-cache blocks across
storage tiers, and the one we used for three revisions of this work, is at a split instant: observe a
prefix, place the blocks that exist, and bill the result against a hindsight oracle. On two production
Mooncake traces this protocol measures almost nothing. Of the oracle's advantage, 94.7\% and 95.9\% sits
on blocks that do not yet exist at the split, so the best possible split-time policy reaches 5.3\% and
4.1\% of it; the share stays at 92--99\% for every split from 0.3 to 0.7 and under tenfold
changes to the latency and token weights. Inside that protocol a better ranker helps monotonically, but only over the
sliver: the strongest model gains under 3\% of the oracle's advantage. Deciding at each block's arrival
instead puts more within reach: under every modelling convention we tried, ranking new blocks at arrival
captures at least three times what a clairvoyant split-time policy can. How a rule ranks them moves the
result by at most 2.1 points; where it ranks them against blocks already seen, a decision a split-time
evaluation never meets, moves it by 18--39. On Mooncake's synthetic control deciding at arrival does not
reliably help. Reactive tiering is consistent with this: LRU, LFU and a fill-once cache
reach 75--99\% and 40--94\% of Belady's hit rate, the best adaptive policy (ARC, LeCaR, CACHEUS) beats
the best of those rules, each chosen per operating point, by at most 4.6 points, and a learned
replacement policy (LRB) stays below ARC, because 76--78\% of blocks are never reused. We give a one-line
diagnostic for the protocol failure, release an artifact that regenerates every number on CPU, and
catalogue the measurement defects we made and caught, including leaks in our own arrival-time experiment
that survived every number check.
\end{abstract}
